Limitations and future work

Known limitations

The design is a verified prototype with deliberate boundaries. Each limitation below is a choice, with a stated consequence.

LimitationConsequenceWhat would be needed
Single-flit packetsEvery guarantee holds for packets that fit in one flit. Real cache lines usually span several flits, which adds head, body and tail handling.VC allocation per packet at the head, VC held until the tail, per-VC packet state, and a review of every invariant and proof
Pre-layout, one-corner timingCritical paths exclude wires, clock-to-q, setup and process variation. Crossover clock periods in the decision rule are indicative, not sign-off.Place and route, parasitic extraction, multi-corner timing, and power
Synthetic trafficFour standard patterns and a three-class protocol model. Real workloads have burstiness, locality and mixes that these do not.Trace-driven or full-system workloads
One compromised tileThe threat model has one attacker and three defined attacks. Collusion, many attackers and unmodeled behavior are open.A broader threat model and matching defenses
No rate limitingA tile that floods well-formed traffic in the correct network slows others without deadlocking. The flood control shows it is not mitigated.A per-tile token bucket at the local port
No confidentiality defensesShared links leak timing. The network does not hide who talks to whom or when.Out of scope; would need partitioning or traffic shaping
Watchdog assumes a deadlock-free protocolOn a deadlock-prone configuration it turns deadlock into message loss.Keep virtual networks on. A watchdog that also checks overall progress would be a research question.
Deadlock freedom not formally provedIt is argued and measured but not proved by a tool for the whole mesh.A temporal proof over a protocol model, or a channel-dependency-graph argument per network

Allocator-bound clockThe VC router meets only about 8 ns in the pipelined form, against 3.3 ns for the simple router.Speculative or split allocation (below)

Candidate next steps

Ordered roughly by how much each would change the design's real-world relevance per unit of work.

  1. Multi-flit wormhole packets. The largest gap between this design and one that could carry real traffic. New hazards appear: interleaving of packets within a VC, VC occupancy across several flits, and tail-triggered release. Credits stay per flit, so the flow-control argument carries over; VC allocation moves to the head flit and must no longer be assumed to follow switch allocation.
  2. Faster allocation. The data states the target: for the two-pass VC router to beat the pipelined 1 VC × 8 router per nanosecond it needs a clock under about 4.4 ns, roughly half of today's 7.94 ns. Three known routes: speculative switch allocation in parallel with VC allocation, allocation split across two cycles with replay of stale nominations, and a wavefront allocator giving a maximal matching in one pass.
  3. Physical implementation. Place and route on sky130 to replace pre-layout estimates, add wire delay to the cross-link path, and measure power. Expect the pipelined designs to gain, since their second stage has slack.
  4. Rate limiting and a wider threat model. A token bucket at the local port closes the flooding gap. A second compromised tile or colluding tiles would test whether per-port defenses still compose.
  5. Stronger formal results. Unbounded proofs of the credit protocol by induction with supporting invariants; a temporal proof of protocol deadlock freedom on an abstract model of the network.
  6. Workload realism. Trace-driven runs from a coherence simulator; other traffic mixes; adaptive routing studies.

Decisions to settle at the start of the HAS

  • Which workload the router is specified for: the three-class coherence protocol as modeled, or a different class set (the class field is 2 bits, so up to four classes fit, but the router currently supports 1 or 3 networks).
  • Single-flit or multi-flit packets, which changes the interface and most of the guarantees.
  • Target clock period and the router variant it implies, using the decision rule.
  • Whether deadlock freedom is a stated requirement (as here) or an assumption passed to the integrator.
  • The security scope: which attacks are in the threat model, and whether the local-port mechanisms are mandatory or configurable.
